Atlas of companion toys

atlas

150 companion toys and robots I could find, from Chatty Cathy (1960) to iMoochi (2026). 61 of them are taken apart into five layers: body, brain, relationship, illusion of life, fate; 89 more are one line each in a census by country. I collected them to build my own: a simple toy with a small neural network right on the chip.

Roots

Ideas and experiments that the toys repeat in one way or another.

Braitenberg vehicles1984 · Valentino Braitenberg, the book Vehicles

A cart, two light sensors, two motors. Wires crossed: it rushes at the light. Wires straight: it turns away. There is no thought inside, but an observer sees character.

BEAM roboticsearly 1990s · Mark Tilden

Robots with no processor: a few transistors wired as nerves, often a solar cell. They crawl towards light and "search". Tilden later designed Robosapien (2004).

Subsumption architecture1986 · Rodney Brooks, MIT

No model of the world: layers of simple behaviours — don't bump, wander, head for a goal. A higher layer overrides a lower one. Brooks co-founded iRobot, the maker of Roomba.

Kismetlate 1990s · Cynthia Breazeal, MIT

A robot head with a face and babble: speech-like sounds without words. People talked to it like a baby. Breazeal later founded Jibo.

The Media Equation1996 · Byron Reeves and Clifford Nass

Stanford experiments: people are polite to computers, take offence at them and flatter them, even knowing they are machines.

Uncanny valley1970 · Masahiro Mori

The more a robot looks like a person, the more likeable it is, up to a point. An almost-human feels creepy.

Pet Rock1975 · Gary Dahl, an ad man (USA) · $3.95alive
Body
Senses nothing and responds with nothing. A stone in a cardboard carrier box with air holes and wood shavings.
Brain
No electronics. The "intelligence" is a care and training manual; the most popular trick is "play dead".
Relationship
Presence and a joke; a gift for adults ◆.
Illusion of life
Pet-shop packaging. A manual written with a straight face. Tricks where doing nothing counts as success. The owner supplies the name and the care ◆.
Fate
Shown in August 1975. 1.3–1.5 million sold in a few months; cost under a cent, 95 cents profit per rock. The fad was over after Christmas 1975. Super Impulse bought the rights in 2022; a licensed A24 version came out in 2023.
For a toy on a chip
The idea of a pet was tested on people without a single chip, with packaging and text. The joke lasted months; a chip has to add what a rock lacks: a response to what the child does.

2-XL1978 / 1992 · Mego (USA); Tiger Electronics from 1992 (USA) · $50–80; tapes $7.95–9.95 eachsilent
Body
About a foot (30 cm) tall, with a tape player inside: an 8-track cartridge in 1978, a cassette in the 1992 Tiger version. It senses only four red buttons on the belly: Question, A (yes, true), B (more info), C (no, false). No microphone is mentioned ◆. It answers with a voice from the tape; the eyes and a red dome flash while it plays. A mains adapter and one tape, "General Information", come in the box.
Brain
Inside, no network. The logic is on the tape. A button press makes the player jump to another track, so the answer seems to follow the question. A Tiger cassette has four tracks (left and right on each side) and the head reads the one that matches the button. How the toy keeps its place on the tape □. No neural network Σ. The voice belongs to the inventor, Michael Freeman; for Mego it went through a synthesizer.
Relationship
A quiz teacher. The toy asks, the child presses A, B or C. A right answer gets praise and sometimes a harder question; a wrong one gets a scolding. Adventure tapes let the child decide what happens to the characters. Mego made 43 tapes Σ (US history, astronomy, math, sports and others). Tiger made about 45, with licences such as Batman and Jurassic Park.
Illusion of life
It answers in its own voice: a scolding, praise, a joke. Questions get harder after right answers. The child's choice changes the story. The robot voice and the flashing eyes do the rest. The answer is a pressed button, so the toy cannot mishear Σ.
Fate
The inventor patented it in 1976; Mego launched it in 1978. Over 200,000 by summer 1979 (a magazine; source of the figure □). Mego dropped it in 1981, or in 1982 after its bankruptcy; sources differ. Tiger relaunched it in 1992 with cassettes and made it until 1995 or 1996. FamilyFun named it best toy of 1992. Tiger sales □.
For a toy on a chip
A dialogue needs neither speech recognition nor a neural network. The child answers with one of four buttons and the toy jumps to another recorded clip. The whole conversation is a graph written in advance. On a chip it is a table of "node and button give the next node" plus clips in flash. There are no hearing errors, and only recorded lines can be said, so every node must be checked before release. The cost is the author's work: Mego needed dozens of tapes. A small network can sit on top, for example to pick the next node by how fast the child answered ◆.
